It was Ben’s birthday, so we went to Hawaii for the first time. I am writing you tips for what to see in the Big Island, and what I learned about Hawaii.

First mistake to make is taking only three days if you plan to both chill and do your fifteen adventure activities. I booked the trip with an unspecified “Hawaii is so close, we can go anytime” feeling. But this is not Europe, where you can just stop by in London or Stockholm or Paris for a weekend. Everything is bigger scale, this is America.

Hawaii is not close to San Francisco, it’s just uncomfortably distant from anything else. It’s in the middle of blue emptiness. Polynesians, the first people who arrived to Hawaii, had to build robust double-hulled canoes, using coconuts and crab nails. They sailed to the islands intentionally and with new hope, to escape overpopulation and droughts in their own land 2000 feet away.

Polynesians crossed over 2,000 miles of ocean in double-hulled canoes called “Waʻa.” Source

We landed at night, got a car, and navigated to Mango Sunset. Closer we got, more Travis Scott on the CarPlay was complemented by strange and intense quacking sounds.

Next morning, we learned from our host Linda  that invasive coqui frogs are responsible for the sound, and can reach 90 decibels. The frogs in fact rule the whole island, having no one who would hunt them, driving some property prices down better than any politician. 

They are small, but loud. Source

I didn’t mention we woke up at a coffee farm. You cant navigate Big Island without car anyway, so I might have as well picked the coolest spot on the island. Even though Hawaii import over 90% of their food, most of the things on our breakfast plates are grown here, at Mango Sunset. 

We kick start the day by surfing lesson, and it’s Ben’s first one. The instructor is a chill guy named Steve who could be paid fortune as a Vibe Specialist at SF tech startup. He spreads positivity and encouragement that makes us actually stand on the surf. Steve throws around phrases like “just follow the waves!” and at the end he awards one lesson attendee as “absolutely best at falling, doing the most incredible falls!”

There is a guy taking pics of us that you can then buy at his website, but the only money I would pay is to have someone remove these from the internet. 

We drive to the next stop, a little puzzled by Hawaiian roads, as the rules seem to be “first-come-first-surf” here. Our next stop is empanadas and gelato, stopping at thrift store. Big Island has plenty of them, but nothing can top Berlin vintage shopping so our standard is too high in this. We drink coffee at Sacred Grounds. The owner sits us at Jesus-themed pillows, and I think he could actually be God himself and we could have been staring into heaven. I am too atheist to evaluate further.

Source

Sacred ground is something you experience a lot on Big Island. Hawaiians value nature and spirituality and protect it by not allowing buildings taller than palm trees or banning billboards. We walk on sacred ground in our next stop, a national historical park. 

We see more predator animals, mongooses. They were brought to Hawaii by sugar industry in attempt to reduce rats eating sugar. Because mongooses are active in a day while rats are nocturnal, it only added another pest on the island, and it now kills local birds.

There are many more examples than coqui frogs and mongooses of accidents and attempts to do something useful, for example bringing beautiful decorative flowers that established themselves under the name “green cancer”. Hawaii only has two native mammals, and there have been so many other invasive species brought to the island, such as mosquitos, pigs, or the British. James Cook, who arrived at Hawaii in 18th century and called them Sandwich Islands, brought diseases, forced “trade offers” and violence. We ride by the monument pointing at the spot where he was killed in this boat. That piece of land is actually officially British land. 

Not just British tried to cook here, Americans arrived too and established Hawai’i as part of US in 20th century. The queens and kings of Hawaii had to face attacks by others trying to bite their piece of Hawaii. Including the attempt of Safety Committee (naming really is everything) to overthrown queen Kiliauiki.

Our day two stars with morning workout and coffee farm tour with Linda. I can’t wait to see how coffee is made and not paying proper attention to little red fruit that Linda makes us collect. As is usual for me, I realize too late these are the coffee. I didn’t know coffee beans look like this. 

Ben is fortunately and unfortunately very opinionated about coffee, so we are leaving the tour with at least two more coffee related items we need to purchase at home. 

We head to Hāpuna beach today because we are tired. You can’t really make mistake with beaches here, everything looks nice, everything feels relaxing, and also sounds that way. We struggle to pronounce most Hawaiian words but to be fair, they also sound quite homogeneous, as if something is off. It is because Hawaiian language only has 13 letters. 

At evening we are embarking a boat and disappearing in the ocean to spot manta rays. Actually, we are not disappearing anywhere, the boat ride is only few minutes, and we are launched next to it, with our snorkels, hanging around one floating board looking funny. We do see some mantas, and they are big majestic creatures illuminating light. I am always skeptical about tourist traps, but it seems like in Hawaii, they are actually worth it. 

Third day is reserved for volcanoes, and when we wake up, there is lava in Kīlauea volcano. Unfortunately it’s two hour drive on the other side of the island.

We drive but miss the volcanic activity by few hours and Ben refuses to leave the overlook spot because he now needs to monitor the situation. However, the lava situation is detected once in 30-40 days, so we have good excuse to return. 

The Mauna Ulu eruption of 1969 generated a 300-metre (1,000 ft)-high lava fountain. Source

It’s still spectacular and we descend into the crater. If Sacred Grounds coffee farm was an entrance to heaven, this could be a gate to hell.

We enjoy the rain and cold. Not all visitors share this experience and there are two types of people: the ones in jackets, and the ones in summer shorts and minis. Big Island can surprise you with how many climates it can serve you during one day, or in this case, one hike. We walk through wet rainforests, and end the adventure in lava tube. We indeed listen to Hobbit audiobook in the car back. 

The next day is the day we flight back to San Francisco because shareholder value doesn’t increase itself. 

We will be coming back as Hawaii feels close to heaven, close to paradise. 

After this trip, I need to make Ben buy ukulele to preserve the atmosphere he created by playing that on the island. And Ben needs to make me switch from my espresso to filter. 

Until next time!
