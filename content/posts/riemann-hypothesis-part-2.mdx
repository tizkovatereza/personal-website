On November 20, Levent Alpöge released a proof of the Riemann hypothesis that he developed with Claude. The next day, the Lean and Mathlib communities [confirmed the proof](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/). I’m a little emotional about this. People have been trying to prove it since 1859. And now there’s a proof you can ask a computer to check. What does that actually mean? Let’s look at it together.

[Part 1 explains the hypothesis](/blog/riemann-hypothesis). The line to remember is the one through ½, where every nontrivial zeta zero has to sit.

## What Lean Checks

Think of the last line of a proof as a claim with a long chain of receipts. In a paper, an author might write “it follows that” and leave a few steps for the reader. In Lean, the proof has to supply enough information for the software to reconstruct the logical steps. Lean’s kernel, its core checker, checks the resulting proof against the exact statement and the rules of its logic. If one step relies on something the argument hasn’t established, the checker needs a proof of that step or an explicit assumption.

For a tiny example, imagine I want to prove that the sum of two even integers is even. I start with two arbitrary even integers and write them as 2a and 2b. Their sum is 2(a + b), which gives a concrete reason it’s even. Lean can check that reasoning for arbitrary integers a and b. We could test a million even pairs on a calculator, but the proof works for the next pair too because it never depended on which pair we picked. The same principle explains why checking a formal proof of the Riemann hypothesis is different from computing more zeta zeros. [How Lean validates proofs](https://lean-lang.org/doc/reference/latest/ValidatingProofs/)

## How They Checked This Proof

![Reviewers checked that the statement matches Mathlib and that the conclusion follows from the stated assumptions](/images/blog/riemann/lean-verification-static.png)

**First, they checked what the theorem says.** Mathlib, Lean’s community-maintained mathematics library, already had a vetted definition of the Riemann hypothesis. The reviewers isolated that statement in a file that depended only on Mathlib. Then they used two independent tools to compare it with the theorem in Alpöge’s submission. Both found that the proof establishes the same statement. This matters because a theorem name alone tells you nothing about what the statement underneath it actually says. [Joint Lean and Mathlib statement](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/)

**Then they checked the argument and its assumptions.** Multiple people rebuilt the library and reproduced the same proof export. Lean’s kernel and independent checkers accepted the exported proof. Andrew Sutherland’s verification records show that the main theorem uses only Lean’s three standard axioms. Checking a standalone export also lets reviewers verify the underlying proof without running code from the submission itself. [Sutherland’s verification files and results](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)

The release also includes the generalized hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions, which extends the same kind of claim to a family of functions. Sutherland’s audit reports results for those theorems as well. [The exact statements checked](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)

## What Claude Did and What We Can Learn Next

Alpöge credits Claude with substantial mathematical contributions and describes guiding the research from a simpler setting toward the one he needed to solve. In his announcement, he also says an attempt at a detailed prose version ran past 250,000 pages. What he released for checking was the formal argument. He expects it can be substantially simplified. [Alpöge’s November 20 announcement](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf)

I keep coming back to the picture from Part 1. Symmetry already told us that a zero off the line would have a mirror partner. The hard part was ruling out the pair altogether. The independent checks tell us the argument succeeds. What I want to read next is the human-scale explanation of which mathematical idea makes that pair impossible, and where else we might be able to use it.
