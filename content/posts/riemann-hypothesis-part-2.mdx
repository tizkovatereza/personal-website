On November 20, Levent Alpöge released a proof of the Riemann hypothesis that he developed with Claude. The next day, the Lean and Mathlib communities [confirmed the proof](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/). I’m a little emotional about this. People have been trying to prove it since 1859. And now there’s a proof you can ask a computer to check. What does that actually mean? Let’s look at it together.

[Part 1 explains the hypothesis](/blog/riemann-hypothesis). The line to remember is the one through ½, where every nontrivial zeta zero has to sit.

## What Lean Does

**Lean is a tool for writing mathematical proofs in a form a computer can check.** You tell it exactly what you want to prove and give it an argument. Lean’s core checker, called the **kernel**, verifies that the conclusion follows from the definitions, theorems, and assumptions the argument uses. [How Lean validates proofs](https://lean-lang.org/doc/reference/latest/ValidatingProofs/)

Mathematicians already have ways to build those arguments. In a **direct proof**, you start from what you know and work toward the claim. In a **proof by contradiction**, you assume the claim is false and show that this leads to something impossible. In **induction**, you prove the first case and show why each case implies the next, like knocking over a row of dominoes.

The same techniques work in Lean. In my thesis, I remember borrowing a step from a paper that said it was obvious. So I wrote that it was obvious too. My supervisor asked me to derive it, and it took a lot more space than the word “obvious.” Lean makes you supply the work behind that sentence, or use a tool that can reconstruct it for the checker.

## What Alpöge and Claude Built

Alpöge says he guided the work from a simpler problem toward the one they needed to solve. He credits Claude with important mathematical ideas along the way. One example was figuring out how to combine tools called *theta lifts* to adapt an earlier method from a different setting. They developed the argument and formalized it as a Lean library of roughly 17 million lines. That includes definitions, existing results written in Lean, and intermediate results the new argument needs. Mathlib supplied a foundation, including the statement of the Riemann hypothesis itself. The 250,000-page figure Alpöge mentions was from an attempt at a prose write-up. What he released was the Lean library. [Alpöge’s announcement](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf) · [The Lean community’s verification report](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/)

### What The Human Brought

Could you and I open Claude and hammer-solve the Riemann hypothesis? I don’t think so. You still have to be a good prompter (haha). Here that meant knowing enough mathematics to see which analogy was worth pursuing and what question to ask next.

We could bring an A4 sheet of notes to my measure theory exam. Anything we wanted. It could tell me what a theorem said. I still had to figure out which theorem would help and what to ask next. I thought of that while reading Alpöge’s account. Stepanov had proved a version of the Riemann hypothesis in a different setting, where polynomials play a role like integers. His method used **multiplicity**. Think of how a polynomial can have the same factor several times, and how derivatives can help you tell when that happens. Alpöge wanted something that could play a similar role over the integers. You can differentiate a polynomial. What would it even mean to differentiate a whole number?

Alpöge guided the search toward a setting where he thought the analogy could work. Claude suggested chaining **theta lifts**, maps that carry information between spaces of automorphic forms. Each step could play a role a bit like adding another layer of multiplicity. Claude also proposed how many steps to use. For a prime sum with cutoff X, try a chain of length about √X. I love that this was a specific answer to a specific mathematical question. [Alpöge’s account of the collaboration](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf)

## What The Proof Actually Does

**The proof rules out zeros to the right of ½. The mirror symmetry from Part 1 then rules them out on the left.** To make the right-hand side empty, Alpöge and Claude turned the zero question into a question about how big a weighted sum over primes and prime powers can get. [Siniša Slijepčević’s outline of the proof](https://hexagonmath.org/pdf/2611.00812v1)

![The proof bounds a weighted prime sum, rules out zeros right of ½, then uses symmetry to rule out zeros on the left](/images/blog/riemann/lean-proof-strategy.png)

1. **Get a bound from the primes.** They smooth the count so it is easier to analyze, and use chains of theta lifts and other tools from number theory to bound it from above. They need that bound on long stretches of scales, with room in the exponent above ½.
2. **Use the bound to rule out zeros on the right.** A Landau-type argument turns those one-sided bounds into a zero-free region. Roughly, a zero farther right than ½ would force the weighted sum to exceed the allowed bound on those stretches.
3. **Use the mirror.** A nontrivial zero to the left of ½ would have a partner to the right. With the right side ruled out, the only place left for a nontrivial zero is the line itself. [A technical diagram and guide to the argument](https://nasqret.github.io/riemann-rev/guide/proof-guide.pdf)

### What Did They Actually Bound?

Take a cutoff of 10. The primes and prime powers below it are 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, and 9. For each number, take the natural log of its base prime and multiply by (10 divided by the number, minus 1). For 4, that’s log(2) × (10/4 − 1), or about 1.04. Adding all the contributions gives about 9.11. Subtract the smooth trend, about 7.25, and the excess is about **1.86**. The bound is a ceiling on that excess. The proof needs ceilings much farther out than 10. [The weighted sum in the technical guide](https://nasqret.github.io/riemann-rev/guide/proof-guide.pdf#page=2)

### Where The Geometry Comes In

If a positive integer is divisible by 4 and by 3, it has to be at least 12. Turn that around. If you know it’s at most 20, there’s a limit to what can divide it. Take logs and those prime factors give you a sum. The proof uses a much more sophisticated version of that idea. The theta lifts come from the theory of automorphic forms and create divisibility at selected primes. An inequality about lattices from arithmetic geometry, called Bost’s slope inequality, limits how much divisibility there can be. That limit helps bound the prime sums. [Technical guide, sections 4–6](https://nasqret.github.io/riemann-rev/guide/proof-guide.pdf#page=4)

## How They Checked This Proof

![The reviewers asked whether the claim was the Riemann hypothesis and whether the argument proved it](/images/blog/riemann/lean-verification-static.png)

**First, did the claim actually say what we mean by the Riemann hypothesis?** Mathlib is a shared library of mathematics written in Lean. It already had an exact statement of the hypothesis for the reviewers to compare with the conclusion in Alpöge’s proof. Two independent tools confirmed they matched.

If you’ve never seen Lean, here’s the same claim both ways.

**In English.** Every nontrivial zero of zeta has real part ½.

**In Lean.** Here’s that claim laid out one condition per line.

```lean
∀ s : ℂ,
  riemannZeta s = 0 →
  (¬ ∃ n : ℕ, s = -2 * ((n : ℂ) + 1)) →
  s ≠ 1 →
  s.re = 1 / 2
```

Read the last line first. `s.re = 1 / 2` says the horizontal coordinate of s is ½. The first line says “for every complex number s.” The arrows say the conclusion follows if all three conditions hold. Zeta has to be zero at s, s can’t be one of the already known zeros −2, −4, −6, …, and s can’t be the pole at 1. One difference is that English can tuck those details into the word *nontrivial*. Lean needs each condition stated precisely. [The formal statements the reviewers checked](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/#what-was-verified)

**Second, did the argument prove that claim?** Multiple people rebuilt the library and reproduced the same proof export. In one run, Sutherland’s team rechecked all 175,187 compiled modules in Lean’s kernel. Independent checkers also verified the full exported proof as a whole. Andrew Sutherland’s audit found that the theorem uses only Lean’s three standard axioms. The export let reviewers check the underlying proof separately from the code that produced it. [Sutherland’s verification records](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)

![Diagram showing the module-by-module Lean replay and independent checks of the exported proof](/images/blog/riemann/lean-module-replay.png)

Some checks zoomed in on one link at a time. An independent reading project isolated the step from the prime-sum bound to the Riemann hypothesis and checked it in two proof checkers. That check takes the bound as a premise. The full proof also supplies the argument for the bound itself. [The isolated checks and their premises](https://github.com/nasqret/riemann-rev#the-two-implications-checked-in-isolation)

The release also covers the generalized hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions, a family of functions related to zeta. Sutherland’s audit includes those statements too. [The exact statements checked](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)

## What I Want To Understand Next

I keep coming back to the picture from Part 1. Symmetry already told us that a zero off the line would have a mirror partner. The hard part was ruling out the pair altogether. The independent checks tell us the argument succeeds. What I want to read next is the human-scale explanation of which mathematical idea makes that pair impossible, and where else we might be able to use it.

I also keep thinking about Lean. It gave Alpöge and Claude a way to write down the mathematics precisely enough that other people could independently check the result. I’m hesitant to call that AGI. The story I see is a mathematician recognizing a promising analogy, Claude contributing ideas, and a formal language making the argument checkable. Maybe that’s me coping as a mathematician. I find it exciting anyway. There’s still mathematics to do, and now we have a new way to do some of it.
