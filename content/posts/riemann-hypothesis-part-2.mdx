On November 20, Levent Alpöge released a proof of the Riemann hypothesis that he developed with Claude. The next day, the Lean and Mathlib communities [confirmed the proof](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/). I’m a little emotional about this. People have been trying to prove it since 1859. And now there’s a proof you can ask a computer to check. What does that actually mean? Let’s look at it together.

[Part 1 explains the hypothesis](/blog/riemann-hypothesis). The line to remember is the one through ½, where every nontrivial zeta zero has to sit.

## What Lean Does

**Lean is a tool for writing mathematical proofs in a form a computer can check.** You tell it exactly what you want to prove and give it an argument. Lean’s core checker, called the **kernel**, verifies that the conclusion follows from the definitions, theorems, and assumptions the argument uses. [How Lean validates proofs](https://lean-lang.org/doc/reference/latest/ValidatingProofs/)

Mathematicians already have ways to build those arguments. In a **direct proof**, you start from what you know and work toward the claim. In a **proof by contradiction**, you assume the claim is false and show that this leads to something impossible. In **induction**, you prove the first case and show why each case implies the next, like knocking over a row of dominoes.

The same techniques work in Lean. The difference is how much you can leave to the reader. On paper, I can write “it follows that” and expect a mathematician to fill in a familiar step. In Lean, that step has to be spelled out or reconstructed by the software so the kernel can check it. Take a tiny direct proof. Two even integers can be written as 2a and 2b. Their sum is 2(a + b), so it’s even. Lean checks the reasoning for arbitrary a and b, which is what makes the proof cover every pair.

## What Alpöge and Claude Built

Alpöge says he guided the work from a simpler problem toward the one they needed to solve. He credits Claude with important mathematical ideas along the way. One example was figuring out how to combine tools called *theta lifts* to adapt an earlier method from a different setting. They developed the argument and formalized it as a Lean library of roughly 17 million lines. That includes definitions, existing results written in Lean, and intermediate results the new argument needs. Mathlib supplied a foundation, including the statement of the Riemann hypothesis itself. [Alpöge’s announcement](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf) · [The Lean community’s verification report](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/)

## How They Checked This Proof

![The reviewers asked whether the claim was the Riemann hypothesis and whether the argument proved it](/images/blog/riemann/lean-verification-static.png)

**First, did the claim actually say what we mean by the Riemann hypothesis?** Mathlib is a shared library of mathematics written in Lean. It already contained a carefully vetted statement of the hypothesis. Reviewers compared that statement with the conclusion in Alpöge’s code. Two independent tools found they matched. It’s like checking the question at the top of an exam before grading the answer. A proof of a statement about only the first billion zeros would be answering a different question. [Joint Lean and Mathlib statement](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/)

**Second, did the argument prove that claim?** Multiple people rebuilt the library and reproduced the same proof export. Lean’s kernel and independent checkers accepted it. Andrew Sutherland’s audit found that the theorem uses only Lean’s three standard axioms. The export let reviewers check the underlying proof separately from the code that produced it. [Sutherland’s verification records](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)

The release also covers the generalized hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions, a family of functions related to zeta. Sutherland’s audit includes those statements too. [The exact statements checked](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)

## What I Want To Understand Next

I keep coming back to the picture from Part 1. Symmetry already told us that a zero off the line would have a mirror partner. The hard part was ruling out the pair altogether. The independent checks tell us the argument succeeds. What I want to read next is the human-scale explanation of which mathematical idea makes that pair impossible, and where else we might be able to use it.
