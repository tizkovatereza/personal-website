## Let’s start with how Lean works

Alpöge did use Claude, but Claude used Lean, a programming language that can check mathematical proofs. I strongly believe the human and robot couldn't have pulled off this proof without being able to codify it in Lean. Computers work much more efficiently with code, and the scale of the new theory needed to be built for proving Riemann Hypothesis was tremendous.

You tell Lean exactly what you want to prove and give it the steps you think prove it. Lean’s built-in proof checker, called the **kernel**, checks that those steps are allowed (each step has to follow from an assumption, a definition, or a result you already know) and that they get you to the claim you started with. [How Lean validates proofs](https://lean-lang.org/doc/reference/latest/ValidatingProofs/)

Say you want to prove that if x = 2, then x + 3 = 5. You can replace x with 2 because that’s what you assumed. Then you’re left with 2 + 3 = 5. The kernel checks that the substitution is justified and that the arithmetic gives you the result you set out to prove.

### What a proof is in math

Mathematicians already have ways to put those steps together.

- **Direct proof.** Start from what you know and work toward the claim.
- **Proof by contradiction.** Assume the claim is false and show that this leads to something impossible.
- **Induction.** Prove the first case and show why each case implies the next, like knocking over a row of dominoes.

The same techniques work in Lean. In my thesis, I remember borrowing a step from a paper that said it was obvious. Naive me thought it could be “obvious” too in my thesis. My supervisor asked me to derive it, and the “obvious” took over a page of mathematical text.

### What Alpöge and Claude built

Alpöge [says](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf#page=1) he guided the work from a simpler problem toward the one they needed to solve. He credits Claude with important mathematical ideas along the way. One example was figuring out how to combine tools called *theta lifts* to adapt an earlier method from a different setting. They developed the argument and formalized it as a Lean library of roughly 17 million lines.

They built on [Mathlib](https://leanprover-community.github.io/), a shared collection of definitions, theorems, and proofs written in Lean. Mathlib already had a definition of the zeta function and spelled out what the Riemann hypothesis says about its zeros. In the first file below, `RiemannHypothesis` is the name Mathlib gives to that statement. Alpöge and Claude supplied the proof, and reviewers could check that it proved the exact statement Mathlib already had. #### What Alpöge brought to the proof

Could you and I open Claude and hammer-solve the Riemann hypothesis? I don’t think so. You still have to be a good prompter. Here that meant knowing enough mathematics to see which analogy was worth pursuing and what question to ask next.

For example, at university we were allowed to bring an A4 sheet of our own notes to our measure theory exam. We could put anything we wanted on it. But it still didn’t help if you couldn’t figure out which theorems to use for which problems.

### What the proof actually does

To understand at least parts of Alpöge’s proof, I would summarize it as three core ideas. First, add up weighted contributions from primes and their powers up to a number you choose (the cutoff), then compare the total with its expected trend. A zero to the right of ½ would make the total overshoot that trend by more than the proof allows as the cutoff grows. Then use geometry to put a ceiling on that sum. Finally, show that a zero off the line would be incompatible with that ceiling. [Siniša Slijepčević’s outline of the proof](https://hexagonmath.org/pdf/2611.00812v1)

#### 1. Measure how far a weighted sum over primes strays

Why add up primes in the first place? The proof needs a number that tracks how the primes are distributed and that would behave differently if the Riemann hypothesis were false. One way to get that number is to give each prime and prime power a score, add those scores, and compare the total with a smooth baseline. That total is what I mean by the prime sum here. The gap above the baseline is what the proof needs to bound.

Say we stop at 10. That means we only include primes and prime powers up to 10. Here, those are 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, and 9. Take 4. It’s on the list because 4 = 2². The rule starts with the natural log of the prime you raised to a power, so for 4 we start with log(2), which is about 0.693.

Then we multiply by a weight that depends on how close 4 is to the cutoff. The rule is to divide the cutoff by the number and subtract 1. Here that’s 10 ÷ 4 − 1 = 2.5 − 1 = 1.5. This gives smaller numbers more weight and makes the weight drop to zero at the cutoff. Multiplying 0.693 by 1.5 gives about 1.04. That’s the amount 4 adds to the total.

Apply the same rule to the other six numbers and add all seven contributions. The total is about 9.11.

Where does the baseline come from? The weighted total grows as we raise the cutoff. The baseline is a formula for its overall growth, so we can subtract that growth and look at what’s left. For a cutoff of `X`, the formula is `X × (log(X) − 1 − γ)`, where γ is the Euler–Mascheroni constant, about 0.5772. It only needs the cutoff. At 10 it gives about 7.25, so the actual total sits about **1.86 above the baseline** (9.11 − 7.25). The bound is a ceiling on that excess. Ten is just an example of a cutoff, the number we count up to. The proof needs an upper bound that holds throughout certain long ranges of cutoffs, and it needs such ranges at arbitrarily large scales. [The weighted sum in the technical guide](https://nasqret.github.io/riemann-rev/guide/proof-guide.pdf#page=2)

#### 2. Build a ceiling using geometry

To put a ceiling on the excess, Alpöge borrowed an idea from a different problem in math. Sergei Stepanov had found a way to limit how many points can lie on a curve by using a polynomial. Alpöge wanted to see if the same kind of reasoning could put a ceiling on a sum over primes.

##### How Stepanov counted points on a curve

In his announcement, Alpöge traces one of the ideas behind the proof to Sergei Stepanov, a mathematician who in 1969 used polynomials to tackle a related problem. Stepanov wanted to put an upper limit on how many points lie on certain curves when you do arithmetic with remainders. Alpöge and Claude were after a different upper limit, this time on a sum involving primes. To see why they reached for Stepanov’s idea, start with what he was counting. [Alpöge’s announcement](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf) · [An introduction to Stepanov’s method](https://www.ias.edu/video/csdm/stepanov)

Imagine working modulo 5, so every calculation ends by taking the remainder after dividing by 5. For the curve **y = x²**, choosing x = 2 gives y = 4. Choosing x = 3 also gives y = 4, because 9 leaves a remainder of 4. So (2, 4) and (3, 4) are two different points on the curve. With only five possible x values, we could list all the points in this example. Stepanov’s method gives a way to bound the count for more complicated curves without listing the points one by one.

The trick is to build a second function on the curve. Every point you want to count has to be a zero of this function, meaning the function is zero there. And you want each of those zeros to be a **repeated** zero. To see why that helps, here’s an ordinary polynomial in one variable.

<div className="not-prose my-6 overflow-x-auto py-2 text-lg sm:text-xl"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="block"><mrow><mi>P</mi><mo>(</mo><mi>t</mi><mo>)</mo><mo>=</mo><msup><mrow><mo>(</mo><mi>t</mi><mo>−</mo><mn>2</mn><mo>)</mo></mrow><mn>3</mn></msup><msup><mrow><mo>(</mo><mi>t</mi><mo>−</mo><mn>5</mn><mo>)</mo></mrow><mn>3</mn></msup></mrow></math></div>

Put in 2 and the first factor becomes zero. Put in 5 and the second becomes zero. Those are two distinct zeros. But the factor (t − 2) appears three times, so we say the zero at 2 has **multiplicity three**. The same goes for 5. Each copy contributes one to the degree, so together those two zeros account for all six degrees of the polynomial.

Now imagine you can build a nonzero polynomial of degree at most 60, and every zero you care about has multiplicity at least three. You don’t even have to know where the zeros are. Each one accounts for at least three degrees, so there can be at most **60 ÷ 3 = 20 distinct zeros**. A twenty-first would require a degree of at least 63.

On a curve, Stepanov uses the same counting idea with a function defined on the curve itself. The hard part is constructing a function that vanishes repeatedly at the points you care about, stays within a known limit on its total zeros counted with multiplicity, and isn’t zero everywhere on the curve. Each point then accounts for several of the available zeros. That’s the idea Alpöge wanted to carry over. What could play the role of a repeated zero when the things you’re counting are primes? [An exposition of Stepanov’s method](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/StepanovsMethod.html)

##### How theta lifts build divisibility

Alpöge wanted something that could do the job of repeated zeros when the objects he cared about were integers and primes. A **theta lift** takes an automorphic form, a function with particular symmetries, and transfers information from it to a new form. In this construction the output of one lift becomes the input of the next. The chain builds up divisibility at selected primes. That divisibility plays the role of repeated vanishing in the earlier argument.

##### What carries over from Stepanov?

- **Counting points on a curve.** Build a function with repeated zeros. A bound on its total zeros, counted with multiplicity, limits how many points it can cover.
- **Bounding a sum over primes.** Chain theta lifts to build divisibility. A geometric inequality limits how much can build up.

Alpöge [says](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf#page=1) he guided the search toward a setting where the analogy could work. Claude suggested using about <math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><msqrt><mrow><mi>X</mi></mrow></msqrt></mrow></math> lifts when the prime sum has cutoff <math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mi>X</mi></mrow></math>. The exact chain and the inequality that controls it are where the technical work is. [The technical guide, section 4](https://nasqret.github.io/riemann-rev/guide/proof-guide.pdf#page=4)

##### How divisibility gives a bound

If a positive integer is divisible by 4 and by 3, it has to be at least 12. Turn that around. If you know it’s at most 20, there’s a limit to what can divide it. Take logs and those prime factors give you a sum. The proof uses a much more sophisticated version of that idea. The theta lifts come from the theory of automorphic forms and create divisibility at selected primes. An inequality about lattices from arithmetic geometry, called Bost’s slope inequality, limits how much divisibility there can be. That limit helps bound the prime sums. [Technical guide, sections 4–6](https://nasqret.github.io/riemann-rev/guide/proof-guide.pdf#page=4)

The proof needs three ingredients to turn this construction into an inequality.

1. **Construct forms with controlled size.** The theta lifts give a way to build these forms.
2. **Show there are enough forms.** Together, they span the space the proof needs.
3. **Bound how small the integral forms can be.** An independent lower bound limits how small a full set of integral forms can be.

Putting those pieces together gives the ceiling on the weighted prime sum. [Reading guide for the shorter proof](https://github.com/lsernau/grh-lean/blob/main/grh/GUIDE.md)

#### 3. Use the ceiling to rule out zeros off the line

The proof combines several related prime sums with positive weights so the effect of a zero survives in the combined sum. The Landau argument then says that a zero to the right of ½ would force the sum to break the ceiling somewhere in every sufficiently large range of the required length. The geometric bound gives ranges on arbitrarily large scales where that doesn’t happen. This rules out zeros to the right of ½. By the mirror symmetry from Part 1, a zero on the left would have a partner on the right, so that side is ruled out too. [Technical guide, sections 2–5](https://nasqret.github.io/riemann-rev/guide/proof-guide.pdf#page=2)

#### A summary in four steps

1. **Build a ceiling.** The theta lifts build up divisibility. Geometry limits how much can build up, so a weighted sum over primes has a ceiling on certain long stretches of cutoffs.
2. **Combine the sums.** The bound arrives for several related prime sums together. They combine them with positive weights so a zero’s signal survives in the combined sum.
3. **A zero on the right would break the ceiling.** The Landau argument says an off-line zero to the right would force the sum above the ceiling somewhere in every sufficiently late stretch. The bound gives stretches where it stays below.
4. **Reflection takes care of the left.** A zero on the left would have a partner on the right. With the right side ruled out, the nontrivial zeros sit on the line through ½.

### How reviewers checked the proof

**First, check that the code states the Riemann hypothesis.** This is a standard check, even when the theorem looks right on screen. A theorem named `RiemannHypothesis` could say that *one* zero lies on the line. Lean could correctly check a proof of that sentence. The Riemann hypothesis says *every* nontrivial zero lies there.

Here, reviewers had a reference they could check against. Mathlib already defines the standard statement using its own definition of zeta. Two independent tools compared the result in Alpöge’s library with that reference and confirmed they say the same thing.

If you’ve never seen Lean, here’s the same claim both ways.

Every nontrivial zero of zeta has real part ½. Here’s how that reads in Lean, one condition per line.

```lean
∀ s : ℂ,
  riemannZeta s = 0 →
  (¬ ∃ n : ℕ, s = -2 * ((n : ℂ) + 1)) →
  s ≠ 1 →
  s.re = 1 / 2
```

Read the last line first. `s.re = 1 / 2` says the horizontal coordinate of s is ½. The first line says “for every complex number s.” The arrows say the conclusion follows if all three conditions hold. Zeta has to be zero at s, s can’t be one of the already known zeros −2, −4, −6, …, and s can’t be the pole at 1. One difference is that English can tuck those details into the word *nontrivial*. Lean needs each condition stated precisely. [The formal statements the reviewers checked](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/#what-was-verified)

**Second, did the argument prove that claim?** Multiple people rebuilt the library and reproduced the same proof export. In one run, Sutherland’s team rechecked all 175,187 compiled modules in Lean’s kernel. Independent checkers also verified the full exported proof as a whole. Andrew Sutherland’s audit found that the theorem uses only Lean’s three standard axioms. The export let reviewers check the underlying proof separately from the code that produced it. A separate sandboxed rebuild produced a byte-for-byte identical export.

Sutherland’s **November 25** run compared all five theorem statements against the reference statements, checked the assumptions, and ran three proof checkers on the original archive. All three accepted it. [Sutherland’s verification records](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)

#### Verification checklist

1. **Check the statement.** Compare the conclusion to Mathlib’s formal statement of the hypothesis.
2. **Replay the modules.** Rebuild the library and recheck 175,187 compiled modules in Lean’s kernel.
3. **Check the export independently.** Give the full proof object to other checkers.

Some checks zoomed in on one link at a time. An independent reading project isolated the step from the prime-sum bound to the Riemann hypothesis and checked it in two proof checkers. That check takes the bound as a premise. The full proof also supplies the argument for the bound itself. [The isolated checks and their premises](https://github.com/nasqret/riemann-rev#the-two-implications-checked-in-isolation)

The release also covers the generalized hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions, a family of functions related to zeta. Sutherland’s audit includes those statements too. [The exact statements checked](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)

## Was it disproved already?

One nontrivial zero off the line would settle this. Two older manuscripts claim that such zeros exist.

Huan Xiao’s January 2025 preprint tries to get a contradiction from two results about weighted prime sums. The step I’d want to see spelled out is equation 3. It takes an oscillation in one count and claims it survives after smoothing. That’s where the argument needs more detail. [Xiao’s preprint, equations 1–3](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202501.0292/v1)

Tatenda Kubalalika’s February 2026 working paper claims that some zeros get arbitrarily close to the right edge of the strip. That would be a much stronger result than finding one off-line zero. Both manuscripts are unreviewed on the sites hosting them. I couldn’t find an independent check that confirms either claim. [Kubalalika’s working paper](https://www.cambridge.org/engage/coe/article-details/698bc7d24770e67d92050e8a)

### What about Masullo’s draft?

Ravi Vakil reported on November 20 that Alessandro Maria Masullo had a separate proposed proof of the generalized hypothesis and that Brian Conrad had been reviewing it. In the released manuscript, section 12.3 still has an instruction to fill in the section. That part is supposed to connect the calculation on the zero side with the one on the prime side. Without it, a reader has an argument with a step left to check. This is a question about Masullo’s proposed proof. Alpöge’s argument uses a different route. ## Conclusion

I actually feel pretty positive about this. Maybe that’s me coping as a mathematician. But the way we do math has been changing for as long as we’ve been doing it. We keep finding ways to get further with the same amount of work.

Think about counting on your fingers versus writing the numbers down. Or drawing a diagram to see why something might be true, then doing what Euclid did and writing out the assumptions and the argument so someone else can follow it. With Lean, you can write the argument precisely enough that a computer can check it too.

That’s what I find exciting about this proof. Alpöge saw a connection to another area of math. Claude helped him work out how to use it. Lean made the argument possible for other people to check independently. It feels like we just got another way to do math, and I’m excited to see how much further we can get with the weird questions we already love asking.
