What do prime numbers have to do with points on a vertical line? We’ll start with primes, explain why we introduce a seemingly unrelated function, and build up to the Riemann hypothesis itself. You don’t need a background in complex analysis. I want to explain what each piece is doing before putting them together.

*On November 20, 2026, Levent Alpöge released a proof developed with Claude. The Lean and Mathlib communities independently verified it the next day. [Read Part 2 for what Lean checked](/blog/riemann-hypothesis-part-2).*

## Where Do Prime Numbers Appear?

Prime numbers are whole numbers greater than 1 divisible only by 1 and themselves. 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19… They are the building blocks of whole numbers. For example,

<Equation>60 = 2² × 3 × 5</Equation>

Every whole number greater than 1 has a unique prime factorization, apart from the order. But the primes themselves appear irregularly. Sometimes they are close together, like 11 and 13. Sometimes there is a much larger gap. One way to see their patterns is to arrange whole numbers in an outward square spiral, then highlight the primes. This is called an [Ulam spiral](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/PrimeSpiral.html). Diagonal patterns start appearing.

![Prime numbers highlighted on an Ulam spiral](/images/blog/riemann/prime-spiral-static.png)

I am an atheist, but seeing that prime numbers form mysterious shapes and patterns is a religious experience for me, reaching the limits of human intuition. We chose the spiral arrangement, so the picture depends on how we display the numbers. Still, the concentrations of primes along some diagonals are interesting mathematical patterns. The Riemann hypothesis concerns another kind of regularity. **How many primes appear as we move further along the number line?**

## Counting Primes

Instead of asking “What is the next prime?”, ask “How many primes are there up to a given number?” We call this count π(x). The π here names a counting function. It has nothing to do with circles.

<Equation>π(10) = 4</Equation>

Because there are four primes up to 10. They are 2, 3, 5, and 7. Imagine drawing this count as a staircase. Every time you pass a prime, the count goes up by one. Mathematicians have smooth formulas that approximate this staircase. The prime number theorem describes the overall growth. The Riemann hypothesis concerns how far the actual count can deviate from a more precise approximation. Keep that picture in mind. **A staircase, a smooth curve, and the difference between them.**

## Meet The Zeta Function

When a problem is difficult, mathematicians often translate it into another language. Instead of studying a list of numbers directly, we build a function connected to those numbers. Then we can use tools for studying functions. The **Riemann zeta function** is one such tool. For some inputs, we can calculate it with this recipe.

<Equation>ζ(s) = 1 + 1/2ˢ + 1/3ˢ + 1/4ˢ + ⋯</Equation>

ζ is the Greek letter zeta. It is just the function’s name. And s is its input. Here is what the recipe does. Take each positive whole number, raise it to the power s, take its reciprocal, and add all those contributions. For example, with s = 2,

<Equation>ζ(2) = 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + 1/16 + ⋯ ≈ 1.645</Equation>

With s = 3,

<Equation>ζ(3) = 1 + 1/8 + 1/27 + 1/64 + ⋯ ≈ 1.202</Equation>

For these real inputs, increasing s makes the contributions from larger numbers shrink faster. Think of s as a setting that changes how strongly the function weights different whole numbers. But why is adding these fractions useful? Because this function has an exact connection to primes. And Riemann found formulas connecting its **zeros**, the inputs where its output becomes zero, to the distribution of primes. Zeta doesn’t directly answer “What is the next prime?” It gives us another way to investigate the whole distribution.

## Where The Primes Enter

Euler discovered this equality,

<Equation>ζ(s) = 1 + 1/2ˢ + 1/3ˢ + 1/4ˢ + ⋯<br />= 1/(1 − 2⁻ˢ) × 1/(1 − 3⁻ˢ) × 1/(1 − 5⁻ˢ) × 1/(1 − 7⁻ˢ) × ⋯</Equation>

The first expression adds contributions from **all positive whole numbers**. The second multiplies factors involving **only primes**. Both expressions equal ζ(s), when the real part of s is greater than 1. We’ll explain “real part” shortly. It is essentially a clever shuffle of terms. Expand each factor into a geometric series, multiply, and every positive whole number appears exactly once because prime factorization is unique. Trust me on this one for now. If you want the details, here are the [Euler-product identities and their conditions](https://dlmf.nist.gov/27.4). Those conditions matter. Infinite sums cannot always be rearranged freely. Here, absolute convergence makes the shuffle valid.

So we already have a connection between two different areas. A fact about multiplication and prime factorization becomes an identity involving infinite sums.

## Why The Original Sum Stops Working

Adding infinitely many terms can give a finite answer.

<Equation>1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + 1/16 + ⋯ = 1</Equation>

To see what an infinite sum equals, add the first few terms and keep going. If the running totals approach one finite number, that number is the value of the sum. We say it **converges**. The zeta sum at s = 2 settles near 1.645. At s = 1, here’s what happens instead.

<Equation>1 + 1/2 + (1/3 + 1/4) + (1/5 + ⋯ + 1/8) + ⋯</Equation>

Start with the first bracket. It has two terms, each at least ¼, so it adds at least ½. The next bracket has four terms, each at least ⅛, so it also adds at least ½. Keep doubling the number of terms in each bracket and you keep gaining at least another ½. The running total grows without bound, even though the individual terms get smaller.

At s = ½, every term is at least as large as its counterpart at s = 1. The running total grows without bound there too. **To find ζ(½), we need a different recipe.** The original sum converges only when the real part of s is greater than 1. The zeta function extends across the complex plane and gives finite values everywhere except at s = 1. Let’s see what that means.

## Giving Numbers Another Direction

Ordinary real numbers sit on a line. Complex numbers give us a second direction. We write them as

<Equation>s = a + bi</Equation>

Here, i² = −1. For this article, picture a and b as coordinates. The **real part**, a, gives the horizontal position. The **imaginary part**, b, gives the vertical position. So 2 + 3i becomes the point (2, 3). Two units right, three units up.

![The point 2 + 3i is two units right and three units up](/images/blog/riemann/complex-plane-static.png)

This is what I love about math. If something is too difficult to look at from one point of view, you can choose a different point of view. An unfamiliar expression becomes a point you can picture. A complex function takes a complex number as input and returns another complex number. So the input and output can each be pictured on their own plane. One distinction to remember is that **input locations and output locations are different things**. Later, when we draw the zeros on a line, we will be drawing inputs.

## Why We Need Analytic Continuation

The original zeta sum gives us a way to calculate ζ(s) when the real part of s is greater than 1. On our picture of the complex plane, that’s everything to the right of the vertical line through 1. But the Riemann hypothesis asks about zeros on the line through **½**. Our recipe stops before we get there. What does ζ even mean on that part of the plane?

**Analytic continuation** is a way to extend a function by finding a recipe that agrees with it where we already know both recipes work. Let’s see what that means with a sum small enough to do by hand.

## One Function With Two Recipes

Start with this infinite sum.

<Equation>1 + x + x² + x³ + ⋯</Equation>

At x = ½, we’re adding 1, then ½, then ¼, and so on. The running totals settle at 2. There’s a second way to get the same answer: divide 1 by 1 − x. To see why, call the sum S. Multiplying S by x shifts every term one place. Subtract xS from S and all the terms cancel except the first 1. So (1 − x)S = 1. This works inside the circle of radius 1 around zero, where the sum converges.

<Equation>1 + x + x² + x³ + ⋯ = 1/(1 − x)</Equation>

Now try x = −2. The running totals of the sum go **1, −1, 3, −5, 11, …**. They never settle. But the second recipe gives 1/(1 + 2) = ⅓. That ⅓ is the value of the *extended function* at −2. The infinite sum still diverges there.

## Why The New Values Are Forced

You might wonder why we’re allowed to pick 1/(1 − x) as the extension. Could we make up some other recipe that gives the same values inside the circle and something different outside it?

The word **analytic** supplies the rule. An analytic function has a convergent power series around each point where it’s defined. A power series centered at a uses powers of x − a, the displacement from that center. The circles below show where each local recipe converges. Start with the series centered at 0, then choose −0.75 as a new center inside the first circle. Because the old recipe already describes the function in a little neighborhood around −0.75, it determines the coefficients of the new series there. The new series reaches −2, which was outside the original circle.

![The disk centered at −0.75 overlaps the original disk and extends far enough to include −2](/images/blog/riemann/continuation-static.png)

The overlap is the important part. We know the recipes agree on a whole patch, which fixes the new power series. If two analytic extensions agree on such a patch, they agree everywhere on the connected region they both cover. That’s the *identity theorem*. It tells us there’s only one answer where a continuation exists. Finding a recipe that actually gets there is a separate step.

## Back To Zeta

For zeta, we can get a glimpse of a new recipe by taking the original sum and subtracting twice its even terms. That changes every even term from a plus to a minus and keeps every odd term as it was. The sum of the even terms is 2⁻ˢ times the original sum. That gives us this identity wherever the original sum converges.

<Equation>(1 − 2¹⁻ˢ) ζ(s) = 1 − 1/2ˢ + 1/3ˢ − 1/4ˢ + ⋯</Equation>

The alternating sum on the right converges at the real input s = ½, because the terms alternate in sign and shrink to zero. The factor multiplying ζ(s) on the left is also nonzero there. So we can divide by it to get a value for ζ(½), even though the original all-positive sum diverges at ½. That’s a concrete example of continuing zeta across the boundary of its first recipe. The full continuation reaches every complex input except s = 1. [NIST’s definition and the alternating-series formula](https://dlmf.nist.gov/25.2)

Now we can ask which inputs in the newly reachable region send us to the output 0. Before we do, there’s one point that the extension still can’t fill in.

## What Happens At A Pole?

Our simpler function has a problem at 1,

<Equation>f(x) = 1/(1 − x)</Equation>

As x approaches 1, the denominator approaches zero.

<Equation>f(0.9) = 10, f(0.99) = 100, f(0.999) = 1000</Equation>

From the other side,

<Equation>f(1.1) = −10, f(1.01) = −100, f(1.001) = −1000</Equation>

This kind of point is called a **pole**. The output’s magnitude grows without bound as the input approaches it. Exactly at 1, there is no finite output. We would be dividing by zero. Being from Prague, I want to compare this to the Golem. In one version of the legend, Rabbi Loew places a parchment carrying the sacred name, the *shem*, into its mouth and brings it to life.

![An illustration of Rabbi Loew placing the shem in the clay Golem’s mouth](/images/blog/riemann/golem.webp)

*An illustration made for this article, inspired by the [Prague Golem legend](https://www.jewishmuseum.cz/novinka/the-golem-of-prague-the-legend-of-rabbi-loew-s-creation/).*

A pole has that “special input where the quiet creature suddenly becomes uncontrollable” feeling. The mathematical meaning is more specific, of course. The function doesn’t become random or alive. Its output grows without bound **as we approach** the special input. Zeta also has a pole at s = 1. Elsewhere, its extended version is analytic. From now on, that extended function is what we mean by zeta.

## What Is A Zero Of A Function?

“Zero” here is also a term. A **zero of a function is an input that makes its output equal zero**. For example, f(x) = x − 3 has a zero at **3**, because f(3) = 0. So a zero of a function doesn’t mean the input number 0. As a loose human analogy, imagine a function measuring your remaining energy after an activity. Your “zero” might be a long hike. Or a glass of wine, after which you shut down for the evening. The hike isn’t the number zero. It is the input that produces zero as the result. For zeta, we want numbers s such that

<Equation>ζ(s) = 0</Equation>

Since those inputs can be complex numbers, we can mark them as points on the input plane.

## What Makes Some Zeros Trivial?

Some zeta zeros occur at −2, −4, −6, −8, and so on. These are called the **trivial zeros**. “Trivial” often means a result follows relatively directly from definitions or established facts. It doesn’t mean you should immediately find it obvious. Here, these zeros follow from an identity called zeta’s functional equation. Their locations are understood. The **nontrivial zeros** are the remaining ones. They lie in the strip where the real part is between 0 and 1. You can find the [precise statement here](https://dlmf.nist.gov/25.10).

## Why Would They Lie On One Vertical Line?

Start with these inputs,

<Equation>½ + 2i, ½ + 5i, ½ − 3i</Equation>

Their heights differ. Their horizontal coordinate is always ½. So they sit directly above or below one another, on the vertical line through ½. Some known zeta zeros are approximately

<Equation>½ + 14.134725i<br />½ + 21.022040i<br />½ + 25.010858i</Equation>

![Three marked zeta zeros share the real coordinate one half](/images/blog/riemann/critical-line-static.png)

The height here belongs to the input. At each marked point, the output is zero. But why would ½ be special? Why not 0.4 or 0.7?

## The Middle Is A Mirror

Zeta has a deep symmetry, expressed through its functional equation. Among other things, the nontrivial zeros are symmetric about the vertical line through ½. If a + bi is a nontrivial zero, then its reflected point (1 − a) + bi is also a zero. So a hypothetical zero with horizontal coordinate 0.3 would have a partner at 0.7, at the same height. They are equally far from ½. The midpoint is special because

<Equation>a = 1 − a implies a = ½</Equation>

A point on that middle line reflects onto itself.

![Hypothetical zeros with real parts 0.3 and 0.7 sit at the same height on opposite sides of one half](/images/blog/riemann/zero-symmetry-static.png)

This gives intuition for **why that particular line is the natural candidate**. It is built into the function’s symmetry. But symmetry alone doesn’t put every zero on the line. Imagine two people standing on opposite sides of a mirror. They can form a symmetric arrangement without either person standing on the mirror itself. Likewise, zeros could theoretically occur in reflected pairs away from the middle. The Riemann hypothesis says that never happens. So the difficult leap is from “the zeros are symmetric around this line” to “every nontrivial zero actually lies on this line.”

## Finally, The Whole Hypothesis

Now we can put the sentence together.

**Every nontrivial zero of the Riemann zeta function has real part ½.**

The word **all** matters. To prove “there exists a prime greater than 100,” one example is enough. Here is 101. Done. But checking a million zeros, or a trillion, doesn’t establish a claim about every zero. There are infinitely many left. Universal claims can have short proofs, and existence claims can be difficult. Still, the difference in what counts as sufficient evidence matters. One example can establish existence. One counterexample can destroy a universal claim.

## Why This Connection Is So Beautiful

Let’s return to why we introduced zeta. Prime numbers belong to a world of whole numbers, divisibility, and multiplication. These are discrete objects. You can list them one by one. Analysis studies functions, limits, infinite series, differentiation, and integration. It gives us ways to reason about continuous behavior. Euler’s product connects those worlds. Unique prime factorization becomes an identity involving an infinite sum and an infinite product. Riemann then takes the connection further. The zeros of a complex function enter explicit formulas for counting primes. Their positions control fluctuations in those counts.

This is what I love about math. Different areas can look like separate subjects until someone finds a connection, and suddenly you can use everything you know about one to understand the other. You are looking at prime numbers and asking where they appear. Then you change the point of view, and the question becomes about a function. Change it again, and you are looking at geometry in the complex plane. The connections are exact enough that proving something about the geometry gives you a theorem about the original numbers.

For me, this is a big part of why abstraction is useful. It gives us more ways to approach the same problem.

## What Would A Proof Change?

It would establish a strong bound on the error in our approximation of prime counts. More precisely, the hypothesis is equivalent to

<Equation>π(x) = Li(x) + O(√x log x)</Equation>

Here, π(x) is the actual prime count, Li(x) is the smooth approximation, and the final term bounds how fast the error can grow. Eventually, the error’s magnitude is at most a fixed constant times √x log x. The [official problem description](https://www.claymath.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/riemann.pdf) explains the connection. Remember the staircase and the smooth curve? This tells us how far apart they can become. It wouldn’t make primes evenly spaced. It would also make results proved assuming RH unconditional. Results requiring generalized versions would need those broader hypotheses established too.

Primes appear in cryptography, but counting primes and factoring a particular large number are different tasks. A proof of RH would not, by itself, supply a fast factoring algorithm or automatically break encryption. And the proof’s methods could be as interesting as the conclusion. Which connection finally makes the argument work? Could the same ideas help elsewhere?

We started with prime numbers, moved through infinite sums and complex analysis, and ended with a geometric question about a line. I love that these ideas can connect so precisely. A geometric restriction on the zeros of a function becomes a guarantee about prime counts.
