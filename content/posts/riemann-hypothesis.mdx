Levent Alpöge announced that he’d proved the Riemann hypothesis with Claude. I wanted to write about it and look into what had actually been proved. It’s a big, emotional moment for a lot of mathematicians. People have been trying to prove this since 1859.

Why does it matter? **A proof puts a precise limit on how far off we can be when we estimate the number of primes up to a given size.** It also settles the assumption behind a lot of results mathematicians had already proved *if* the hypothesis was true. And the wild part, to me, is that the limit on this counting error comes from where points sit on a vertical line. [Clay’s explanation of the Riemann hypothesis](https://www.claymath.org/millennium/riemann-hypothesis/)

How many primes are there below a million? You can count them. But what if you want to know how that count behaves as you go further and further out? How good an estimate can you get, and how far off could it be?

In 1859, Bernhard Riemann wrote a paper about that counting problem. He started with something Euler had noticed earlier. An infinite sum over **all** the whole numbers could also be written as a product over **just** the primes. Riemann took the function behind that sum, called zeta, and asked what it does when the inputs are complex numbers. He found that the places where zeta becomes zero show up in a formula for counting primes. [Riemann’s 1859 paper](https://www.claymath.org/collections/riemanns-1859-manuscript/)

So why does a sum of fractions have anything to do with primes in the first place? Let’s build up to it.

## Where Do Prime Numbers Appear?

Prime numbers are whole numbers greater than 1 divisible only by 1 and themselves. 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19… They are the building blocks of whole numbers. For example,

<Equation>60 = 2² × 3 × 5</Equation>

Every whole number greater than 1 has a unique prime factorization, apart from the order. But the primes themselves appear irregularly. Sometimes they are close together, like 11 and 13. Sometimes there is a much larger gap. One way to see their patterns is to arrange whole numbers in an outward square spiral, then highlight the primes. This is called an [Ulam spiral](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/PrimeSpiral.html). Diagonal patterns start appearing.

![Prime numbers highlighted on an Ulam spiral](/images/blog/riemann/prime-spiral-static.png)

I am an atheist, but seeing that prime numbers form mysterious shapes and patterns is a religious experience for me, reaching the limits of human intuition. We chose the spiral arrangement, so the picture depends on how we display the numbers. Still, the concentrations of primes along some diagonals are interesting mathematical patterns. The Riemann hypothesis concerns another kind of regularity. **How many primes appear as we move further along the number line?**

## Counting Primes

Instead of asking “What is the next prime?”, ask “How many primes are there up to a given number?” We call this count π(x). The π here names a counting function. It has nothing to do with circles.

<Equation>π(10) = 4</Equation>

Because there are four primes up to 10. They are 2, 3, 5, and 7. Imagine drawing this count as a staircase. Every time you pass a prime, the count goes up by one. Mathematicians have smooth formulas that approximate this staircase. The prime number theorem describes the overall growth. The Riemann hypothesis concerns how far the actual count can deviate from a more precise approximation. Keep that picture in mind. **A staircase, a smooth curve, and the difference between them.**

## What The Zeta Function Does

**The Riemann zeta function takes a number s and returns a number ζ(s).** Its input can be a point on the complex plane. When the output is zero, we call that input a **zero of zeta**.

Here’s the catch. The easiest formula for zeta works only to the right of the line through 1. **Analytic continuation keeps the values we already know and gives us values farther left, where the zeros we care about are.** Let’s start with the formula we can actually calculate.

### The Original Sum

At s = 2, take the reciprocal of every positive whole number squared and add them up. For other values of s, use s as the exponent.

<Equation>ζ(s) = 1 + 1/2ˢ + 1/3ˢ + 1/4ˢ + ⋯<br />ζ(2) = 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + 1/16 + ⋯ ≈ 1.645</Equation>

An infinite sum has a value when its running total approaches a finite number. We say it **converges**. At s = 2, the running total settles near 1.645. At s = 1, it keeps growing. You can see why by grouping the fractions.

<Equation>1 + 1/2 + (1/3 + 1/4) + (1/5 + ⋯ + 1/8) + ⋯</Equation>

The first bracket has two terms of at least ¼ each. The next has four terms of at least ⅛ each. Every bracket adds at least ½, and the brackets go on forever. At s = ½ the terms are even larger, so that sum grows without bound too. To find ζ(½), we need a different formula.

### Where The Original Sum And Extended Zeta Agree

Think of a complex number as a point on a grid. **2 + 3i** is two units right and three units up. The horizontal coordinate, 2, is called the **real part**. That’s all we need to read the figure below.

A **sum converges** when its running totals settle toward one number. A **function is defined** at an input when it assigns that input a value. At s = ½, the original zeta sum diverges, but extended zeta is defined there. We can get its value from another formula.

**The original sum converges when the real part of s is greater than 1.** Extended zeta gives exactly the same values there and is defined everywhere else except at s = 1.

### How Analytic Continuation Works

Try the smaller example **1 + x + x² + x³ + ⋯**. At x = ½, the sum settles at **2**. The formula **1/(1 − x)** also gives **2**. In fact, for any x between −1 and 1, multiplying the convergent sum by (1 − x) cancels everything except 1, so the formulas agree throughout that interval.

At x = −2, the original sum starts **1 − 2 + 4 − 8 + ⋯** and the running total jumps around forever. The second formula gives **⅓**. That’s a value of the extended function at −2. The original sum still diverges there.

Analytic continuation works on this principle. Find a formula that agrees with the original where both work, then use it at new inputs. The word **analytic** imposes a precise rule. The function has to be expressible near each point as a convergent power series. That rule makes the extension unique on a connected region.

### A Formula That Reaches ½

For zeta, one formula that reaches new inputs uses alternating plus and minus signs. **To get ζ(½),** take the following convergent sum and divide by 1 − √2.

<Equation>ζ(½) = (1 − 1/√2 + 1/√3 − 1/√4 + ⋯) / (1 − √2)</Equation>

Where the original sum converges, you get the alternating sum by subtracting twice the even terms. The even terms alone are 2<sup>−s</sup> times the original sum, so the alternating sum equals (1 − 2<sup>1 − s</sup>) times zeta there. At ½, the alternating sum still converges and the multiplier is 1 − √2. This formula illustrates how we can reach ½. Other formulas cover the rest of the extended domain. [NIST’s definition and alternating-series formula](https://dlmf.nist.gov/25.2)

Now we can ask which inputs in the newly reachable region send us to the output 0. Before we do, there’s one point that the extension still can’t fill in.

## What Happens At A Pole?

Our simpler function has a problem at 1,

<Equation>f(x) = 1/(1 − x)</Equation>

As x approaches 1, the denominator approaches zero.

<Equation>f(0.9) = 10, f(0.99) = 100, f(0.999) = 1000</Equation>

From the other side,

<Equation>f(1.1) = −10, f(1.01) = −100, f(1.001) = −1000</Equation>

This kind of point is called a **pole**. The output grows without bound in magnitude as the input approaches it. Exactly at 1, there’s no finite value. Zeta also has a pole at s = 1.

![Rabbi Loew placing the parchment in the clay Golem’s mouth](/images/blog/riemann/golem.webp)

*I’m from Prague, where there’s a [legend about a clay Golem](https://www.jewishmuseum.cz/novinka/the-golem-of-prague-the-legend-of-rabbi-loew-s-creation/) that comes to life when a parchment is placed in its mouth. I like thinking of the input as that parchment. Get close to one particular value and a quiet-looking function turns into an uncontrollable creature.*

## What Is A Zero Of A Function?

“Zero” here is also a term. A **zero of a function is an input that makes its output equal zero**. For example, f(x) = x − 3 has a zero at **3**, because f(3) = 0. So a zero of a function doesn’t mean the input number 0. As a loose human analogy, imagine a function measuring your remaining energy after an activity. Your “zero” might be a long hike. Or a glass of wine, after which you shut down for the evening. The hike isn’t the number zero. It is the input that produces zero as the result. For zeta, we want numbers s such that

<Equation>ζ(s) = 0</Equation>

Since those inputs can be complex numbers, we can mark them as points on the input plane.

## What Makes Some Zeros Trivial?

Some zeta zeros occur at −2, −4, −6, −8, and so on. These are called the **trivial zeros**. “Trivial” often means a result follows relatively directly from definitions or established facts. It doesn’t mean you should immediately find it obvious. Here, these zeros follow from an identity called zeta’s functional equation. Their locations are understood. The **nontrivial zeros** are the remaining ones. They lie in the strip where the real part is between 0 and 1. You can find the [precise statement here](https://dlmf.nist.gov/25.10).

## Why Would They Lie On One Vertical Line?

Start with these inputs,

<Equation>½ + 2i, ½ + 5i, ½ − 3i</Equation>

Their heights differ. Their horizontal coordinate is always ½. So they sit directly above or below one another, on the vertical line through ½. Some known zeta zeros are approximately

<Equation>½ + 14.134725i<br />½ + 21.022040i<br />½ + 25.010858i</Equation>

![Three marked zeta zeros share the real coordinate one half](/images/blog/riemann/critical-line-static.png)

The height here belongs to the input. At each marked point, the output is zero. But why would ½ be special? Why not 0.4 or 0.7?

## The Middle Is A Mirror

Zeta has a deep symmetry, expressed through its functional equation. Among other things, the nontrivial zeros are symmetric about the vertical line through ½. If a + bi is a nontrivial zero, then its reflected point (1 − a) + bi is also a zero. So a hypothetical zero with horizontal coordinate 0.3 would have a partner at 0.7, at the same height. They are equally far from ½. The midpoint is special because

<Equation>a = 1 − a implies a = ½</Equation>

A point on that middle line reflects onto itself.

![Hypothetical zeros with real parts 0.3 and 0.7 sit at the same height on opposite sides of one half](/images/blog/riemann/zero-symmetry-static.png)

This gives intuition for **why that particular line is the natural candidate**. It is built into the function’s symmetry. But symmetry alone doesn’t put every zero on the line. Imagine two people standing on opposite sides of a mirror. They can form a symmetric arrangement without either person standing on the mirror itself. Likewise, zeros could theoretically occur in reflected pairs away from the middle. The Riemann hypothesis says that never happens. So the difficult leap is from “the zeros are symmetric around this line” to “every nontrivial zero actually lies on this line.”

## Finally, The Whole Hypothesis

Now we can put the sentence together.

**Every nontrivial zero of the Riemann zeta function has real part ½.**

**Every** is doing a lot of work here. Check a trillion of them and there are still infinitely many left. Find one off the line and the hypothesis is false.

## Why This Connection Is So Beautiful

Let’s return to why we introduced zeta. Prime numbers belong to a world of whole numbers, divisibility, and multiplication. These are discrete objects. You can list them one by one. Analysis studies functions, limits, infinite series, differentiation, and integration. It gives us ways to reason about continuous behavior. Euler’s product connects those worlds. Where the original sum converges, zeta can also be written as a product with one factor for each prime.

<Equation>ζ(s) = 1/(1 − 2⁻ˢ) × 1/(1 − 3⁻ˢ) × 1/(1 − 5⁻ˢ) × ⋯</Equation>

Expand each factor as a geometric series and multiply. Because every positive whole number has a unique prime factorization, each term in the original sum appears exactly once. [Euler’s product formula](https://dlmf.nist.gov/27.4)

Riemann then takes the connection further. The zeros of a complex function enter explicit formulas for counting primes. Their positions control fluctuations in those counts.

This is what I love about math. Different areas can look like separate subjects until someone finds a connection, and suddenly you can use everything you know about one to understand the other. You are looking at prime numbers and asking where they appear. Then you change the point of view, and the question becomes about a function. Change it again, and you are looking at geometry in the complex plane. The connections are exact enough that proving something about the geometry gives you a theorem about the original numbers.

For me, this is a big part of why abstraction is useful. It gives us more ways to approach the same problem.

## What Would A Proof Change?

Remember the staircase of actual prime counts and the smooth curve we use to estimate it? The Riemann hypothesis tells us how far apart they can get. The two statements are mathematically equivalent. Prove that every nontrivial zero lies on the line through ½ and you get this bound on the error. Prove the bound and you get the statement about the zeros back.

<Equation>π(x) = Li(x) + O(√x log x)</Equation>

Here, **π(x)** is how many primes there actually are up to x. **Li(x)** is our smooth estimate. The **O(√x log x)** part says how far off the estimate can be. Once x is large enough, the absolute difference stays below some fixed constant times √x log x. Think of a band around the estimate. It can get wider as x grows, but it gets much smaller relative to the number of primes we’re counting. We’re learning how much the primes can bunch up or thin out in the cumulative count. The formula doesn’t tell us which numbers are prime. [Enrico Bombieri’s write-up for the Clay Mathematics Institute states the equivalence on page 4](https://www.claymath.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/riemann.pdf#page=4).

### Where The ½ Shows Up

Here’s the bit I find wild. In a formula for a weighted count of primes and prime powers, every nontrivial zero contributes a correction. A zero at **a + bi** gives a term involving **x raised to that complex number**. The imaginary part makes the correction oscillate. The real part a gives the power of x in its size.

<Equation>|x<sup>a + bi</sup>| = x<sup>a</sup></Equation>

So if the real part is **½**, that power is **x<sup>½</sup> = √x**. A zero farther to the right would have a larger exponent. The hypothesis says every nontrivial zero has the square-root exponent. Bounding the corrections together and translating the weighted count back to the ordinary prime count gives the √x log x bound above. That’s where a position on the zeta graph becomes a limit on the error in counting primes. [The explicit formula for the weighted count](https://dlmf.nist.gov/25.16.E2)

Primes appear in cryptography, but counting primes and factoring a particular large number are different tasks. A proof of RH would not, by itself, supply a fast factoring algorithm or automatically break encryption. And the proof’s methods could be as interesting as the conclusion. Which connection finally makes the argument work? Could the same ideas help elsewhere?

We started with prime numbers, moved through infinite sums and complex analysis, and ended with a geometric question about a line. I love that these ideas can connect so precisely. A geometric restriction on the zeros of a function becomes a guarantee about prime counts.
