Levent Alpöge announced that he’d proved the Riemann hypothesis with Claude. I wanted to write about it and look into what had actually been proved. It’s a big, emotional moment for a lot of mathematicians. People have been trying to prove this since 1859.

Why does it matter? **A proof puts a precise limit on how far off we can be when we estimate the number of primes up to a given size.** It also settles the assumption behind a lot of results mathematicians had already proved *if* the hypothesis was true. And the wild part, to me, is that the limit on this counting error comes from where points sit on a vertical line. [Clay’s explanation of the Riemann hypothesis](https://www.claymath.org/millennium/riemann-hypothesis/)

Here’s the claim we’re working toward. **Every nontrivial zero of the Riemann zeta function has real part ½.** Let’s unpack what those words mean and then come back to the primes.

How many primes are there below a million? You can count them. But what if you want to know how that count behaves as you go further and further out? How good an estimate can you get, and how far off could it be?

In 1859, Bernhard Riemann wrote a paper about that counting problem. He started with something Euler had noticed earlier. An infinite sum over **all** the whole numbers could also be written as a product over **just** the primes. Riemann took the function behind that sum, called zeta, and asked what it does when the inputs are complex numbers. He found that the places where zeta becomes zero show up in a formula for counting primes. [Riemann’s 1859 paper](https://www.claymath.org/collections/riemanns-1859-manuscript/)

So why does a sum of fractions have anything to do with primes in the first place? Let’s build up to it.

## Where Do Prime Numbers Appear?

Prime numbers are whole numbers greater than 1 divisible only by 1 and themselves. 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19… They are the building blocks of whole numbers. For example,

<Equation>60 = 2² × 3 × 5</Equation>

Every whole number greater than 1 has a unique prime factorization, apart from the order. But the primes themselves appear irregularly. Sometimes they are close together, like 11 and 13. Sometimes there is a much larger gap. One way to see their patterns is to arrange whole numbers in an outward square spiral, then highlight the primes. This is called an [Ulam spiral](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/PrimeSpiral.html). Diagonal patterns start appearing.

![The first 25 numbers arranged in a spiral, followed by a larger view showing only primes and highlighting one diagonal](/images/blog/riemann/prime-spiral-static.png)

*Start at 1 and write the whole numbers in a spiral. Zoom out and mark just the primes to see the diagonal patterns.*

I am an atheist, but seeing that prime numbers form mysterious shapes and patterns is a religious experience for me, reaching the limits of human intuition. We chose the spiral arrangement, so the picture depends on how we display the numbers. Still, the concentrations of primes along some diagonals are interesting mathematical patterns. The Riemann hypothesis concerns another kind of regularity. **How many primes appear as we move further along the number line?**

## Counting Primes

There are 25 primes up to 100. Draw the count as a staircase and each prime takes you one step up. The smooth curve gives an estimate of about 30 at the same cutoff. So it’s about five too high there. The Riemann hypothesis puts a limit on how far apart those two can get as we count farther out.

![A staircase of actual prime counts and a smooth estimate, with the gap marked at 100](/images/blog/riemann/prime-count-staircase.png)

## What The Zeta Function Does

A function takes an input and gives you an output. Feed me chocolate, I turn happy. Zeta's recipe involves a lot more fractions.

Zeta takes a number we call **s**. Plot **2 + 3i** by going two units right and three up. The two is the real part, the three is the imaginary part, and **s** is the whole point. Writing **2** is shorthand for **2 + 0i**.

### The Original Sum

<Equation>ζ(s) = 1 + 1/2<sup>s</sup> + 1/3<sup>s</sup> + 1/4<sup>s</sup> + ⋯</Equation>

Use the same **s** in every exponent. At **s = 2**, that gives 1 + ¼ + ⅑ + 1/16 + ⋯. The running total settles near 1.645. That’s what **converges** means. A function is **defined** at an input if it gives that input a value.

At **s = 1** and **s = ½**, the running total keeps growing. At **s = 1 + i** it also fails to settle, though the terms now turn in different directions. The sum converges exactly when the real part of **s** is greater than 1. That means strictly right of the vertical line through 1.

### How Analytic Continuation Works

Why go farther? Riemann was looking for a way to count primes. The zeros that give corrections to the estimate live in the strip between real parts 0 and 1. The original sum can’t reach them.

**Match first, then extend.** Find a different formula that gives the old answers everywhere on the right and still works at new inputs. One trick is to flip the sign of every other term. At ½, the alternating terms cancel enough for the sum to settle. A factor in the denominator undoes the change so the formula still matches the original on the right. [Derivation of the alternating formula](https://dlmf.nist.gov/25.2.E3)

Why is there only one extension? Two analytic functions that match on an open patch have to keep matching throughout the connected region where both work. I love this. Those answers on the right pin down the answers farther left. [MIT’s notes on the identity theorem](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-04-complex-variables-with-applications-spring-2018/af8c9c78c3915aaf3fd1695f8d32a3b3_MIT18_04S18_topic13.pdf#page=2)

### A Formula For The Whole Extended Function

Here’s what an actual recipe for the whole plane looks like. **Hasse’s formula works at every complex input except 1.** You can use the short sum to the right of 1 and this one everywhere else it’s defined.

<Equation>ζ(s) = [1/(s − 1)] ∑<sub>n=0</sub><sup>∞</sup> [1/(n + 1)] ∑<sub>k=0</sub><sup>n</sup> (−1)<sup>k</sup> C(n, k) (k + 1)<sup>1 − s</sup></Equation>

Here C(n, k) means “n choose k.” For a quick example, use **s = 0**. The original sum is 1 + 1 + 1 + ⋯. In Hasse’s formula the first two groups give 1 and −½, and the rest cancel. Divide by −1 and you get **ζ(0) = −½**. [Hasse’s formula, equation 11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.02044#page=5)

### A Quick Recap Of The Two Recipes

In both cases, the input can be complex. Write **s = a + bi**. The real part **a** tells you how far left or right the input is. The imaginary part **b** tells you how high or low it is. The output **ζ(s)** can also be complex.

1. **The original sum works when a > 1.** For example, s = 2 + 3i works because its real part is 2. The height, 3, has no effect on whether the sum converges.
2. **Extended zeta works everywhere except at s = 1.** At 2 + 3i it gives exactly the original sum’s answer. At ½ + 3i it gives an answer too, even though the original sum diverges there. **The difference is which inputs have an answer.** On their shared region, the answers are identical.

**The two vertical lines have different jobs.** The line through 1 marks the boundary of the original sum. The line through ½ is where the Riemann hypothesis says the nontrivial zeros lie. Analytic continuation lets us ask which inputs there give an output of zero.

**A quick side note about the pole.** As s approaches 1 + 0i, the magnitude of ζ(s) grows without bound. At that exact input there’s no finite output. The hypothesis is about the inputs where the output actually equals zero.

![Rabbi Loew placing the parchment in the clay Golem’s mouth](/images/blog/riemann/golem.webp)

*I’m from Prague, where there’s a [legend about a clay Golem](https://www.jewishmuseum.cz/novinka/the-golem-of-prague-the-legend-of-rabbi-loew-s-creation/) that comes to life when a parchment is placed in its mouth. I like thinking of the input as that parchment. Get close to one particular value and a quiet-looking function turns into an uncontrollable creature.*

## What Is A Zero Of A Function?

Imagine a glass of wine is my input. I get tired and shut down. My output is zero. In that very scientific model of me, the glass of wine is a zero.

A **zero of zeta** is an input that lands at the origin of the **output** grid. That output is **0 + 0i**. Zero across, zero up. It’s where the two axes cross.

![Two inputs, minus two and minus four, both map to zero at the intersection of the output axes](/images/blog/riemann/zeta-zero-map.png)

For example, −2 and −4 both land on that same dot. When we ask where the zeros sit, we go back to the **input** grid. That’s where the line through ½ lives.

**A side note on “nontrivial.”** The hypothesis says every **nontrivial** zero has real part ½. The obvious family, once you know where to look, is −2, −4, −6, and so on. Mathematicians already know exactly where those zeros are. The hypothesis is about the rest, in the strip between real parts 0 and 1. [The two kinds of zeros](https://dlmf.nist.gov/25.10)

## Why ½?

The real part is the horizontal coordinate. Saying all the nontrivial zeros have real part ½ means they all sit somewhere on the vertical line through ½. Here are three known examples.

<Equation>½ + 14.134725i<br />½ + 21.022040i<br />½ + 25.010858i</Equation>

![Three marked zeta zeros share the real coordinate one half](/images/blog/riemann/critical-line-static.png)

*Inputs. Each dot gives the same output, zero.*

I’m used to symmetry around zero, where the reflection sends 3 to −3. Here the relation built into zeta pairs the real coordinate **a** with **1 − a**. A zero at 0.3 would have a partner at 0.7, at the same height. The one coordinate that reflects onto itself is ½. That’s why ½ is the natural line to look at. The examples give us a reason to suspect the pattern continues. The symmetry still allows pairs off the line, and ruling out every such pair is the hard part.

![Hypothetical zeros with real parts 0.3 and 0.7 sit at the same height on opposite sides of one half](/images/blog/riemann/zero-symmetry-static.png)

*Inputs. If either hypothetical point sent zeta to zero, its mirror partner would too.*

## Why This Connection Is So Beautiful

Let’s return to why we introduced zeta. Prime numbers belong to a world of whole numbers, divisibility, and multiplication. These are discrete objects. You can list them one by one. Analysis studies functions, limits, infinite series, differentiation, and integration. It gives us ways to reason about continuous behavior. Euler’s product connects those worlds. Where the original sum converges, zeta can also be written as a product with one factor for each prime.

<Equation>ζ(s) = 1/(1 − 2⁻ˢ) × 1/(1 − 3⁻ˢ) × 1/(1 − 5⁻ˢ) × ⋯</Equation>

Expand each factor as a geometric series and multiply. To get the term for **12**, pick 1/4<sup>s</sup> from the factor for 2, 1/3<sup>s</sup> from the factor for 3, and 1 from the others. Their product is 1/12<sup>s</sup>, because 12 = 2² × 3. Each whole number appears exactly once in the expanded product, because each has a unique prime factorization. [Euler’s product formula](https://dlmf.nist.gov/27.4)

### What Are We Correcting?

Say we want to count the primes up to **one million**. The smooth estimate, Li(1,000,000), gives about **78,628**. There are actually **78,498** primes up to that point. The estimate is about **130 too high**. At another cutoff it could be too low. A correction is a term we add or subtract to account for part of that gap.

Here’s how the zeros come in. Starting with Euler’s product, mathematicians take the derivative of its logarithm. The product side then gives a sum over primes and prime powers. The zeta side gives ζ′(s)/ζ(s), which has a pole at each zero of ζ(s). When mathematicians turn that expression into a formula for a weighted prime count, the poles from the nontrivial zeros contribute correction terms. The formula starts with a smooth trend, then adds contributions that can push the count up or down. [The explicit formula for the weighted prime count](https://dlmf.nist.gov/25.16.E2)

This is what I love about math. Different areas can look like separate subjects until someone finds a connection, and suddenly you can use everything you know about one to understand the other. You are looking at prime numbers and asking where they appear. Then you change the point of view, and the question becomes about a function. Change it again, and you are looking at geometry in the complex plane. The connections are exact enough that proving something about the geometry gives you a theorem about the original numbers.

For me, this is a big part of why abstraction is useful. It gives us more ways to approach the same problem.

## What Would A Proof Change?

Remember the staircase of actual prime counts and the smooth curve we use to estimate it? The Riemann hypothesis tells us how far apart they can get. The two statements are mathematically equivalent. Prove that every nontrivial zero lies on the line through ½ and you get this bound on the error. Prove the bound and you get the statement about the zeros back.

<Equation>π(x) = Li(x) + O(√x log x)</Equation>

Here, **π(x)** is how many primes there actually are up to x. **Li(x)** is our smooth estimate. The **O(√x log x)** part says how far off the estimate can be. Once x is large enough, the absolute difference stays below some fixed constant times √x log x. Think of a band around the estimate. It can get wider as x grows, but it gets much smaller relative to the number of primes we’re counting. We’re learning how much the primes can bunch up or thin out in the cumulative count. The formula doesn’t tell us which numbers are prime. [Enrico Bombieri’s write-up for the Clay Mathematics Institute states the equivalence on page 4](https://www.claymath.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/riemann.pdf#page=4).

### Where The ½ Shows Up

Here’s the bit I find wild. In a formula for a weighted count of primes and prime powers, every nontrivial zero contributes a correction. A zero at **a + bi** gives a term involving **x raised to that complex number**. The imaginary part makes the correction oscillate. The real part a gives the power of x in its size.

<Equation>|x<sup>a + bi</sup>| = x<sup>a</sup></Equation>

So if the real part is **½**, that power is **x<sup>½</sup> = √x**. A hypothetical zero with real part ¾ would give a factor of x<sup>¾</sup>, which grows faster. The hypothesis says every nontrivial zero has the square-root exponent. Bounding the corrections together and translating the weighted count back to the ordinary prime count gives the √x log x bound above. That’s where a position on the zeta graph becomes a limit on the error in counting primes. [The explicit formula for the weighted count](https://dlmf.nist.gov/25.16.E2)

Primes appear in cryptography, but counting primes and factoring a particular large number are different tasks. A proof of RH would not, by itself, supply a fast factoring algorithm or automatically break encryption. And the proof’s methods could be as interesting as the conclusion. Which connection finally makes the argument work? Could the same ideas help elsewhere?

We started with prime numbers, moved through infinite sums and complex analysis, and ended with a geometric question about a line. I love that these ideas can connect so precisely. A geometric restriction on the zeros of a function becomes a guarantee about prime counts.
