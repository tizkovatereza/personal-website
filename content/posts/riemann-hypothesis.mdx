[Part 2 · Was It Proven?](/blog/riemann-hypothesis-part-2)

Levent Alpöge announced a Claude-assisted proof of the Riemann hypothesis on November 20. Lean and Mathlib reviewers reported that the formal proof passed independent checks. Clay still lists the problem as unsolved as of November 21. I wanted to write about it and look into what had actually been proved. It’s a big, emotional moment for a lot of mathematicians. People have been trying to prove this since 1859.

**Why count primes in the first place?** In 1859, Riemann was trying to understand how often primes show up as numbers get bigger. Gauss had already guessed a smooth estimate for the count. Riemann found a way to explain the bumps in the actual count using zeta. Today, knowing how dense primes are helps you work out how many candidates you might have to try when looking for a large prime, for example when generating an RSA key. [Riemann’s 1859 paper](https://www.claymath.org/collections/riemanns-1859-manuscript/) · [NIST’s prime-generation standard](https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.FIPS.186-5)

We can already compute exact counts. If we could do it cheaply at huge scales, we could subtract two counts to see exactly how many primes are in a range, or find the millionth prime by looking for where the count first reaches a million. **The Riemann hypothesis answers a different question. How far off can our estimate get as we count farther out?** [An algorithm for exact prime counts](https://websites.umich.edu/lagarias/doc/compute.pdf)

Here’s the claim we’re working toward. **Every nontrivial zero of the Riemann zeta function has real part ½.** Okay, but why are we talking about a function when we want to count primes?

The hypothesis ties a limit on that gap to the line through ½. We’ll come back to how that works. [Riemann’s 1859 paper](https://www.claymath.org/collections/riemanns-1859-manuscript/)

## Where Do Prime Numbers Appear?

Prime numbers are whole numbers greater than 1 divisible only by 1 and themselves. 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19… They are the building blocks of whole numbers. For example,

Every whole number greater than 1 has a unique prime factorization, apart from the order. But the primes themselves appear irregularly. Sometimes they are close together, like 11 and 13. Sometimes there is a much larger gap. One way to see their patterns is to arrange whole numbers in an outward square spiral, then highlight the primes. This is called an [Ulam spiral](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/PrimeSpiral.html). Diagonal patterns start appearing.

*Same spiral, zoomed out. The six green dots are primes next to each other on a diagonal.*

I am an atheist, but seeing that prime numbers form mysterious shapes and patterns is a religious experience for me, reaching the limits of human intuition. We chose the spiral arrangement, so the picture depends on how we display the numbers. Still, the concentrations of primes along some diagonals are interesting mathematical patterns. The Riemann hypothesis concerns another kind of regularity. **How many primes appear as we move further along the number line?**

## Counting Primes

There are 25 primes up to 100. Draw the count as a staircase and each prime takes you one step up. The smooth curve gives an estimate of about 30 at the same cutoff. So it’s about five too high there. The Riemann hypothesis puts a limit on how far apart those two can get as we count farther out.

## What The Zeta Function Does

The Riemann hypothesis says that **every nontrivial zero of the zeta function has real part ½.**

A function takes an input and gives you an output. Feed me chocolate, I turn happy. Zeta's recipe involves a lot more fractions.

Zeta takes a number we call **s**. Plot **<math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mn>2</mn><mo>&#x0002B;</mo><mn>3</mn><mi>i</mi></mrow></math>** by going two units right and three up. The two is the real part, the three is the imaginary part, and **s** is the whole point. Writing **2** is shorthand for **<math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mn>2</mn><mo>&#x0002B;</mo><mn>0</mn><mi>i</mi></mrow></math>**.

### How Zeta Is Defined

The original definition of the zeta function is this infinite sum. It defines ζ(s) when **Re(s) > 1**, meaning the real part of the input is greater than 1.

Use the same **s** in every exponent. At **<math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mi>s</mi><mo>&#x0003D;</mo><mn>2</mn></mrow></math>**, that gives 1 + ¼ + ⅑ + 1/16 + ⋯. The running total settles near 1.645. That’s what **converges** means. A function is **defined** at an input if it gives that input a value.

At **<math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mi>s</mi><mo>&#x0003D;</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></math>** and **<math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mi>s</mi><mo>&#x0003D;</mo><mstyle displaystyle="false" scriptlevel="0"><mfrac><mrow><mn>1</mn></mrow><mrow><mn>2</mn></mrow></mfrac></mstyle></mrow></math>**, the running total keeps growing. At **<math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mi>s</mi><mo>&#x0003D;</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></math> + i** it also fails to settle, though the terms now turn in different directions. The sum converges exactly when **Re(s) > 1**. That means strictly right of the vertical line through 1.

### What Is Extended Zeta?

**Extended zeta gives the same answers wherever the original sum converges. It also gives values on and to the left of the line through 1, with one exception.** At the single point 1 + 0i, zeta has a pole and no finite value.

From here on, ζ means the full function.

You can plug any complex number except 1 into Hasse’s formula. Try it on the right of the line and you get exactly the same answer as the original sum.

**Where did those coefficients come from?** The idea is to work with differences between neighboring powers. Subtract two neighbors, then subtract two neighboring differences. That second step gives the pattern 1, −2, 1. Keep going and you get the alternating binomial coefficients in the first line. The derivation shows that the weighted groups add up to the original zeta value once you divide by s − 1. Those groups also give a convergent series at inputs where the original sum fails. [Hasse’s formula and its derivation, equation 11](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.02044#page=5)

The first line makes one group of terms, called Aₙ(s). The second adds those groups after dividing each by n + 1. The stacked n and k mean “n choose k.” Try **<math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mi>s</mi><mo>&#x0003D;</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow></math>**. The original sum is 1 + 1 + 1 + ⋯. In Hasse’s formula the first two groups give 1 and −½, and the rest cancel. Divide by −1 and you get **<math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mi>&#x003B6;</mi><mo stretchy="false">&#x00028;</mo><mn>0</mn><mo stretchy="false">&#x00029;</mo><mo>&#x0003D;</mo><mo>&#x02212;</mo><mstyle displaystyle="false" scriptlevel="0"><mfrac><mrow><mn>1</mn></mrow><mrow><mn>2</mn></mrow></mfrac></mstyle></mrow></math>**.

In the diagram, blue marks where the original sum works. Green marks where the extension adds values. **Real part 1 is the whole vertical line. The input 1 means <math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>&#x0002B;</mo><mn>0</mn><mi>i</mi></mrow></math>, a single point on that line.** Start at 1 on the horizontal axis and go straight up or down. Your horizontal coordinate stays fixed. At <math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>&#x0002B;</mo><mi>i</mi></mrow></math>, one step above the pole, extended zeta has a finite value.

### How Analytic Continuation Works

Why go farther? Riemann was looking for a way to count primes. The zeros that give corrections to the estimate live in the strip between real parts 0 and 1. The original sum can’t reach them.

How do you find a formula that does this? One way to see the idea is to flip the sign of every other term. At ½, the alternating terms cancel enough for the sum to settle. A factor in the denominator makes this formula match the original on the right. [Derivation of the alternating formula](https://dlmf.nist.gov/25.2.E3)

Why is there only one extension? Two analytic functions that match on an open patch have to keep matching throughout the connected region where both work. I love this. Those answers on the right pin down the answers farther left. [MIT’s notes on the identity theorem](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-04-complex-variables-with-applications-spring-2018/af8c9c78c3915aaf3fd1695f8d32a3b3_MIT18_04S18_topic13.pdf#page=2)

### The Two Zeta Recipes

Both recipes take the whole complex number **s** as input. **Re(s)** is its horizontal coordinate.

1. **The original sum works when Re(s) > 1.** For example, 2 + 3i works because its real part is 2. The 3 tells you the height of the input.
2. **Extended zeta works everywhere except at <math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mi>s</mi><mo>&#x0003D;</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></math>.** At <math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mn>2</mn><mo>&#x0002B;</mo><mn>3</mn><mi>i</mi></mrow></math> it gives exactly the original sum’s answer. At <math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mstyle displaystyle="false" scriptlevel="0"><mfrac><mrow><mn>1</mn></mrow><mrow><mn>2</mn></mrow></mfrac></mstyle><mo>&#x0002B;</mo><mn>3</mn><mi>i</mi></mrow></math> it gives an answer too, even though the original sum diverges there. **The difference is which inputs have an answer.** On their shared region, the answers are identical.

**The two vertical lines have different jobs.** The line through 1 marks the boundary of the original sum. The line through ½ is where the Riemann hypothesis says the nontrivial zeros lie. Analytic continuation lets us ask which inputs there give an output of zero.

As s approaches <math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>&#x0002B;</mo><mn>0</mn><mi>i</mi></mrow></math>, the magnitude of ζ(s) grows without bound. At that exact input there’s no finite output. The hypothesis is about the inputs where the output actually equals zero.

## What Is A Zero Of A Function?

Imagine a glass of wine is my input. I get tired and shut down. My output is zero. In that very scientific model of me, the glass of wine is a zero.

A **zero of zeta** is an input that lands at the origin of the **output** grid. That output is **<math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mn>0</mn><mo>&#x0002B;</mo><mn>0</mn><mi>i</mi></mrow></math>**. Zero across, zero up. It’s where the two axes cross.

The Riemann hypothesis says that **every nontrivial zero of zeta has real part ½.** So the number ½ describes where those inputs sit on the grid. Their output is zero.

![Two inputs, minus two and minus four, both map to zero at the intersection of the output axes](/images/blog/riemann/zeta-zero-map.png)

For example, −2 and −4 both land on that same dot. When we ask where the zeros sit, we go back to the **input** grid. That’s where the line through ½ lives.

**A side note on “nontrivial.”** The hypothesis says every **nontrivial** zero has real part ½. The obvious family, once you know where to look, is −2, −4, −6, and so on. Mathematicians already know exactly where those zeros are. The hypothesis is about the rest, in the strip between real parts 0 and 1. [The two kinds of zeros](https://dlmf.nist.gov/25.10)

For zeros, that symmetry, together with reflection across the real axis, pairs a horizontal coordinate <math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mi>a</mi></mrow></math> with <math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>&#x02212;</mo><mi>a</mi></mrow></math> at the same height. [The completed zeta symmetry](https://dlmf.nist.gov/25.4.E3)

Think of 0.3 and 0.7. They add up to 1, so their midpoint is ½. Every reflected pair has this same midpoint. The only horizontal coordinate that stays put is the one that satisfies <math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mi>a</mi><mo>&#x0003D;</mo><mn>1</mn><mo>&#x02212;</mo><mi>a</mi></mrow></math>, which gives <math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mi>a</mi><mo>&#x0003D;</mo><mstyle displaystyle="false" scriptlevel="0"><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mn>2</mn></mfrac></mstyle></mrow></math>. That’s why the center is ½. Symmetry still allows off-line pairs. Ruling those out is what the hypothesis asks us to do.

## How The Primes Get Into Zeta

Go back to the original sum. It has a term for every positive whole number. Euler showed that when **Re(s) > 1**, the same sum equals a product with one factor for each prime.

<div className="not-prose my-6 overflow-x-auto py-2 text-lg sm:text-xl"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="block"><mrow><mrow><mi>&#x003B6;</mi><mo stretchy="false">&#x00028;</mo><mi>s</mi><mo stretchy="false">&#x00029;</mo><mi>&</mi><mo>&#x0003D;</mo><mn>1</mn><mo>&#x0002B;</mo><mfrac><mrow><mn>1</mn></mrow><mrow><msup><mn>2</mn><mi>s</mi></msup></mrow></mfrac><mo>&#x0002B;</mo><mfrac><mrow><mn>1</mn></mrow><mrow><msup><mn>3</mn><mi>s</mi></msup></mrow></mfrac><mo>&#x0002B;</mo><mo>&#x022EF;</mo><mspace linebreak="newline" /><mi>&</mi><mo>&#x0003D;</mo><mfrac><mrow><mn>1</mn></mrow><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>&#x02212;</mo><msup><mn>2</mn><mrow><mo>&#x02212;</mo><mi>s</mi></mrow></msup></mrow></mfrac><mo>&#x000B7;</mo><mfrac><mrow><mn>1</mn></mrow><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>&#x02212;</mo><msup><mn>3</mn><mrow><mo>&#x02212;</mo><mi>s</mi></mrow></msup></mrow></mfrac><mo>&#x000B7;</mo><mfrac><mrow><mn>1</mn></mrow><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>&#x02212;</mo><msup><mn>5</mn><mrow><mo>&#x02212;</mo><mi>s</mi></mrow></msup></mrow></mfrac><mo>&#x022EF;</mo></mrow></mrow></math></div>

This is **Euler’s product formula**. The reason it works is that every whole number greater than 1 can be written as a product of primes in exactly one way, apart from order. For example, <math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="inline"><mrow><mn>12</mn><mo>&#x0003D;</mo><msup><mn>2</mn><mn>2</mn></msup><mo>&#x000D7;</mo><mn>3</mn></mrow></math>. The 2 factor supplies 1/4<sup>s</sup>, the 3 factor supplies 1/3<sup>s</sup>, and multiplying those gives the term 1/12<sup>s</sup> in the sum. Every number gets its one term this way. [Euler’s product and its derivation](https://dlmf.nist.gov/27.4)

## Counting Primes Up To A Million

The staircase steps up by one at each prime. The smooth curve, called **Li(x)**, adds up the expected density of primes as you move along the number line. Near a large number, that density is roughly one prime per natural log of that number.

Li is short for *logarithmic integral*. Here we use the convention that starts at Li(2) ≈ 1.045 and adds the area under the reciprocal of the natural log.

At **one million**, we can get the exact answer by listing the primes, for example with a sieve. There are **78,498**. Evaluate the smooth formula at the same cutoff and you get about **78,628**. So Li is about **130 too high** there. At another cutoff it could be too low.

A correction is something we add or subtract to bring an estimate closer to the count. The adjustment at a million is about −130 overall. To see how a zero can supply one piece of an adjustment, it helps to start with a different way of counting. ### Counting With Weights

To count primes up to 11, go through the whole numbers one by one. Add **1** when the number is prime and **0** when it isn’t. So 4 adds zero because it has a divisor other than 1 and itself.

The **weighted count** adds the natural log of the base prime for each prime or prime power. So 2, 4, and 8 each add log 2. A number like 6 adds zero because it has two different prime factors.

The weighted count has a simple starting estimate. Up to 125, start at **125**. Near a large cutoff, primes show up roughly once per log of that number, and each prime adds roughly that same log as its weight. Those two effects balance, so the weighted total grows about as fast as the cutoff.

### How The Zeros Give Corrections

Euler’s product is zeta written with one factor per prime. It agrees with the original sum when **Re(s) > 1**.

Working with this product gives a way to calculate the weighted count. The formula starts with the cutoff and then adjusts it. Each pair of zeros at opposite heights contributes a wave. That wave can raise the estimate at one cutoff and lower it at another.

## Where The ½ Shows Up

The horizontal position of a zero tells us how fast its wave can grow. A zero with real part **½** gives a wave whose limit grows like the **square root of the cutoff**. A zero at **¾** would allow a faster-growing wave. The hypothesis says every nontrivial zero is on the ½ line, so every one of these waves has the square-root scale. [The formula connecting zeros and the weighted count](https://dlmf.nist.gov/25.16.E2)

## What The Hypothesis Says About The Counting Error

To get back to the ordinary staircase, drop the steps at higher prime powers and undo the log weights so each prime counts once. Mathematicians can control the combined effect of the corrections through that calculation. The result is a bound on the gap we started with, between the smooth estimate and the actual number of primes. For large cutoffs, the gap can grow at most on the scale of **the square root of the cutoff times its logarithm**. [The prime-counting error bound](https://www.claymath.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/riemann.pdf#page=4)
